## How to read this report

- **Real pitch decks were scarce.** I opened no complete deck slide by slide, with one partial exception. The Peloton 2011 deck exists publicly, but I only saw a third-party slide-by-slide description (Upmetrics). Verkada's 2017 deck appears as one image on a First Round page. I could not read the image, only its caption. Failory pages for Owlr, Angelcam and Gestoos have no slide text. Most evidence below is therefore press, company sites, YC pages and investor memos.
- **Quotes are second-hand.** Page content was read through a summarizing fetch tool, so quoted fragments were extracted by that tool, not by me from raw HTML. Re-verify every quote against the live URL before it goes into a deck or application.
- **Evidence tags.**
  - Unmarked URLs were opened (fetched).
  - **[snippet]** means the fact appeared only in a search-result summary and the page was not opened. Treat it as a lead, not a source.
  - **[inferred]** means my inference.
- **Blocked or failed fetches** (I did not work around them): Wayback Machine (blocked by the tool), businesswire.com and cspdailynews.com (403), classpass.com/partners (403), zippin.com (see "Not found / dropped").

## Group A: camera / CV in someone else's venue

### Verkada
1. **Existing infrastructure.** Its founding pitch was new consumer-grade hardware replacing legacy gear. The site now says "Eliminate NVRs and servers" and "Bridge Command to existing cameras, alarm sensors, and door hardware". So retrofit came later, as a bridge, not as the pitch.
2. **Privacy.** This is the most specific privacy page I found.
   - Facial recognition, vehicle history and audio are "disabled out of the box" and must be enabled by an admin.
   - Customers are "the gatekeepers of their data", and Verkada "does not sell" it.
   - Customer data is not used to train models without opt-in.
   - Face blur is available but not the default, because footage is "unaltered" for investigations.
   - Retention is admin-set per camera, "30 to 365 days".
3. **Two-sided value.** Not applicable (single buyer).
4. **Pricing.** Hardware plus a required license. Examples: indoor CD22 at $799, CD63 at $2,099, "Additional license required". Some SKUs bundle a 1-year license. Source: pricing page.
5. **Pre-revenue traction.** About 100 prototype cameras built from Raspberry Pis and given to "friends and businesses". Installing for Beverly Hills Equinox led to a relationship of "8+ years". The founders spent about a year talking to customers and investors before founding, per First Round.
6. **Market sizing.** No evidence found.
7. **Earliest one-liner.** "Imagine if buildings protected the people inside using consumer technology." (First Round page; I cannot confirm it is the deck's exact wording.)

### Flock Safety
1. **Existing infrastructure.** The opposite. Solar, battery and LTE hardware is pitched as "installed anywhere" (YC page), and the 2019 article says it "runs on solar power and leverages cellular communications". It avoids dependence on the customer's grid or network instead of reusing their cameras.
2. **Privacy.**
   - 2018: "Your neighborhood 100% owns the data." "Flock will not share, sell, or access your data." Admins can disable plate reading.
   - Current trust page: "Flock cameras do not use facial recognition technology", "Every search is recorded".
   - No retention period appears on the trust page, and it is vague on data-sharing terms.
   - **Lesson [inferred]:** a strong ownership claim at seed became a liability later. Claim only what is enforceable.
3. **Two-sided value.** In 2018 the buyers were neighborhoods, HOAs and municipalities. Police were not named as a buyer in the Crunchbase article. Police became central by 2019 ("300 U.S. cities"). Not an end-user-app model.
4. **Pricing.** $1,500 per camera per year on a two-year contract, install and maintenance included (website, 2018). $2,000 per camera per year in 2019 (LTE, cloud, maintenance, software included). The 2019 framing was "about one-tenth the price of a traditional ALPR camera" ($25,000 unit, nearly $250,000 installed).
5. **Pre-revenue traction.** Seed was $120k led by YC. The founder described an arrest within 60 days (a Georgia Tech-type account, **[snippet]**). The 2018 case study was a $3,000 bike theft solved in an Atlanta neighborhood. Claimed "25-50% crime reduction", with no customer count in the article.
6. **Market sizing.** No evidence found in what I opened.
7. **One-liner (YC page).** "The first public safety operating system that eliminates crime." Founder story: break-ins in his own neighborhood plus a claim that "over 80% of property crime goes unsolved".

### Density
1. **Existing infrastructure.** Self-installable, wall-powered sensor over a doorway. 2015: "The sensor is mounted at waist-height in a doorway." 2016: "self-installable, Wi-Fi powered". Current: "self-installable", "People count is finally plug & play".
2. **Privacy.** 2015 founder framing: "real-time, accurate, cheap and anonymous". "Density's sensors count everyone who goes in and doesn't access any additional information." 2016: does not identify people, uses infrared depth, and the logic is "counting plus ones and minus ones". It is a short, concrete mechanism claim, not a certification list.
3. **Two-sided value.** 2015 pitch: "designed to benefit both types of stakeholders" (consumers and small businesses). The consumer side was people checking whether a coffee shop is full.
4. **Pricing.** 2015: hardware free, customers "will pay for access to data". 2016: sensor free, $45 per sensor per month, billed annually. Hardware cost about $95 (2015). Now: $229 sensor plus $8/month per room, $2.50/month per desk.
5. **Pre-revenue traction (2015, seed $200k).**
   - Cafe Kubal (Syracuse) installed one.
   - Workfrom (Portland) piloting.
   - ASUC planned sensors at **University of California gyms** from Aug 2015.
   - Talks with a NYC QSR chain, a nonprofit, and SF and Austin cafes and bars.
   - Fortune noted accuracy "has not yet been vetted".
   - Series A (2016): homeless shelters, Ivy League colleges, theme parks, Uber (NYC GM quoted).
6. **Market sizing.** No evidence found.
7. **Earliest framing.** "real-time, accurate, cheap and anonymous" (Fortune 2015). 2016 line: "If we succeed, it will be boring - like electricity." Per the HackerNoon account, a first pitch to investors was "a little ... dense", and Calacanis's feedback was "Get to the product".

### Standard Cognition / Standard AI
1. **Existing infrastructure.**
   - 2017 coverage: registers products "from camera feed".
   - 2018 TechCrunch: works with "security cameras that have a bit more power than typical cameras". Other coverage said light-touch ceiling cameras, with no layout changes.
   - **2026 site (the pivot):** VISION "uses your existing cameras", will "assess your existing camera coverage", "deployed at the edge with minimal compute requirements", and the banner "Turn your cameras into clarity".
   - The shift from purpose-installed checkout to analytics on existing cameras was driven by layoffs and a strategy change (CSP, **[snippet]**).
2. **Privacy.** 2018: anonymizes data, no facial recognition, non-cloud with only model updates sent to stores. 2026: "never uses facial recognition", "Your video stays local while our algorithms transform it into privacy-safe data". No retention claim.
3. **Two-sided value.** Shoppers use an app (2018). Now: retailers and CPG brands, with analytics as the product.
4. **Pricing.** No evidence found ("Book a Demo").
5. **Pre-revenue traction.** Product launched Aug 2017, $5M seed, "in talks with retailers about pilots", planned a US retailer launch the following year. In July 2018 no customers were named: "focused on releasing the platform to each retail partner that signs on".
6. **Market sizing.** 2018: aimed at the "long tail" of smaller retailers, no figures.
7. **Earliest one-liner (YC).** "AI-powered checkout for retail." It also described itself as "an artificial intelligence platform that allows buyers to grab what they want without having to go to a cashier."

### AiFi
1. **Existing infrastructure.** 2018: "can be retrofitted onto existing stores", and a small store "might only need one or two cameras". 2026: "often use the ceiling-mounted security cameras you already have", possibly "a few additional off-the-shelf cameras", avoids "unnecessary hardware or rewiring", activated remotely "within hours" of hardware install. It admits possible extra cameras.
2. **Privacy.** "Anonymous by default, GDPR-compliant, and camera-first." Lists GDPR, ISO 27001, CCPA. "Does not store personal data"; identity features are modular.
3. **Two-sided value.** Operator-focused. "Eliminate fraud", "Improved on-shelf availability", "Unlocked 24/7 opening hours".
4. **Pricing.** No evidence found. 2018 said grocers have limited budgets and costs will fall.
5. **Pre-revenue traction.** $4M raised (2018). A 50,000 sq ft pilot at an unnamed major grocer, to be announced later. Later: Aldi South Group testimonial.
6. **Market sizing.** No evidence found.
7. **Earliest framing.** "a scalable, checkout-free system similar to Amazon Go". Now: "Your Physical Space Made Intelligent."

### Grabango (cautionary)
1. **Existing infrastructure.** "designed to retrofit existing stores easily and with practically no awareness from customers" (2024 CEO interview). Computer vision rather than shelf sensors was its cost argument versus Amazon.
2. **Privacy.** Not addressed in the interview.
3. **Two-sided value.** Operator and shopper (QR scan at a front kiosk).
4. **Pricing.** No evidence found.
5. **Traction.** Pilots at 7-Eleven, Circle K, GetGo, MAPCO, Coen Markets, Chevron. Aldi was its first grocery deal (one Chicago-area store, April 2024). It then shut down because it "was unable to raise the funding needed". Retailers showed "declining interest in pricey store technology". Most c-store testers chose cheaper self-checkout kiosks.
6. **Market sizing.** None.
7. **One-liner.** Not found.
**Lesson:** a long list of pilots did not become paid rollouts. Pilot-to-paid conversion was the failure point.

### Gym-camera / CV startups (closest direct comparables)

**VPixel Fitness (Agmis)**
1. Turns "existing gym cameras into an intelligent coaching and retention system" using "regular video cameras already present in most gym environments", with "No wearables. No sensors attached to equipment". Setup starts with a camera coverage audit.
2. "fully anonymized in compliance with European GDPR", works "without storing personally identifiable video footage". It also says video monitoring is "sensitive" and gyms should tell members. The 2019 launch article made no privacy claims.
3. Operators get trainer alerts when form breaks down plus churn flags; members get a phone app with summaries and streaks. Configurable down to "trainer-notification-only".
4. Pricing: none stated beyond "SaaS" with low upfront hardware. No customers or results are named in either source.
- This is the closest existing claim to Spottr's. It dates to 2019 launch news, and I saw no customer proof. **Do not claim to be first.**

**Exersight (Germany, 2025)**
1. Installs its own cameras, a computer and network parts ("hardware is installed fixed and discreetly"). Not an existing-camera model.
2. The page says nothing about footage storage or identifiability. Members need no extra devices after a one-time check-in.
3. Members get form feedback; operators get "zusätzlichen Kontext" for personal coaching; useful for staffless or discount studios.
4. No pricing, no named studios. Development site: ELAN Wennigsen. Winner of the milon Innovation Hub 2025 Bootcamp; funded in part by the German economics ministry.
- Tagline: "Fast wie PersonalTraining. Nur besser."

**Kemtai (Israel)**
- "No Sensors or Wearables Required", "Works on any device with a camera".
- "Processing is done on the edge device, and video is not recorded." Claims FDA-listed, CE, GDPR and HIPAA, all self-reported without audit links.
- Buyer is B2B (health systems, digital health platforms, life sciences). No prices. About ten partner logos. Metrics (72% adherence, 33% cost reduction) are internal data.
- Tagline: "Anyone. Anywhere. Any Platform."

**Sency**
- Sells an SDK that partners embed. Integration "about a day" **[snippet]**. Claims "tens of companies with over 1.5 million members".
- Partner outcomes: 300% revenue growth at a CrossFit app, 15% higher workout completion, up to 10x at WODProof.
- No privacy discussion or pricing. Recommends starting with an A/B test.

**Perch**
- Rack-mounted 3D camera sold to teams. $4M round in 2022, about one-third of NFL teams as customers **[snippet]**. Not an existing-camera model.

I found no other B2B vendor that sells member workout tracking from a gym's existing cameras with named customers and prices.

## Group B: operator pays, members get the app

### ClassPass (2014 seed, $2M)
- **Two-sided value (best example).**
  - Studios: integrates with the studio's POS so new guests' details are already there. Positioned against Groupon / LivingSocial "peaked traffic" that "didn't last"; the model builds repeat visits.
  - Users: variety across studios, no registration, prepaid classes.
  - Instructor testimonial: classes "consistently full and/or sold-out".
- **Pricing.** $99/month for 10 classes at any participating studio.
- **Traction at seed.** 65+ boutique studios in NYC and 20+ in Boston. Reservations grew from 20,000 to 100,000 after the Sept 2013 launch. Still in beta with a waitlist.
- **Origin / pivot.** It started as Classtivity, a B2B class-management idea, and found pay-per-class "might not be sticky enough". **[snippet]** for the B2B-to-consumer pivot detail; the article itself states the stickiness point.
- **Tagline.** None in the article; summary line: "bringing value to athletes and local gyms".

### Mindbody
- **Pricing.** Starter "starting at $79 USD/month per location". Higher tiers are quote-only. Branded app is an add-on (not on Starter). The marketplace listing is free; the fee is 20% of a new client's first purchase, capped at $30, charged only when the app brings a client you wouldn't have otherwise had. Prices rise with locations, not staff.

### Glofox
- **Seed (Jan 2016, EUR 500k).** Retainer of EUR 80-150/month. "custom-branded mobile app for each individual client". 10 full-time staff. No customer count in the article.
- **Now.** Essential EUR 220, Boost EUR 300, Elite EUR 515 per month. Branded App is an add-on for the two lower plans. Only the "ABC Insights" add-on is explicitly "$79/per location/mo".

### ABC Fitness, Hapana
- ABC does not publish pricing (**[snippet]**; Capterra's $0.01 per user is a placeholder). Hapana is a white-label CRM and member-app platform for fitness brands (BFT, F45, UFC Gym and others). Funding: $17M in Aug 2024, $7.25M in Jan 2026 (**[snippet]**). No pricing or early-stage material found.

### Toast (Bessemer memo, 2016)
- **Market sizing (best example).** About 1M addressable US restaurants and bars × about $5,000 ARR per location (about $10,000 with modules) gives a $5-10B market. Near-term focus: 300-400k larger independents. "a little more than 1% market penetration" would be a $100M revenue company.
- **Pricing.** Subscription, payments margin, hardware and services. ARR per location about $5,300; inventory add-on $75/month; implementation underpriced.
- This is an investor memo from a $24M round, not a seed deck. Use the method, not the stage.

### EGYM (Genius)
- Headline: "AI for the entire gym. Personalized training plans, tailored to every member and every club."
- Operator ROI lines: "Increase revenue through higher completion rates, lower drop-outs and stronger membership retention." An ROI calculator and demo request, no price, no Genius-specific numbers.
- Members get personalized workouts and visual progress. Pricing model for gyms not found.

## Lessons for Spottr

For each theme: the strongest patterns, what backs them, and what a pre-seed company needs before using them.

### 1. "Runs on existing infrastructure"
- **Pattern A: say it plainly, then show the install step.** Standard AI: "uses your existing cameras" paired with "assess your existing camera coverage". VPixel: a "camera coverage audit". AiFi: "often use the ceiling-mounted security cameras you already have", then admits "a few additional off-the-shelf cameras".
  - *Needs:* a documented test on at least one real gym's existing camera feed. Spottr's `public/demo-data/` clips may count; confirm they came from gym-owned cameras, not new mounts.
- **Pattern B: stage the infrastructure claim.** Peloton's 2011 deck put "run software on existing bikes first, then custom hardware" as a rollout. Verkada now says "Bridge Command to existing cameras".
  - *Needs:* honesty about camera limits (angle, resolution, coverage). AiFi's concession reads as credible.
- **Cautionary: Standard Cognition.** Early coverage said "security cameras with a bit more power"; purpose-installed ceiling cameras did the work. The company later rebranded to "existing cameras" analytics (2024), after pivoting away from checkout. **[inferred]** Overclaiming "no new hardware" invites a pivot later.
- **Cautionary: VPixel (2019).** Spottr cannot claim to be the first with existing gym cameras. Avoid "first" and "only"; it is already claimed on VPixel's side, though with no proof shown.

### 2. Privacy
- **Pattern A: short, mechanical, verifiable.** Density: "counting plus ones and minus ones". Standard AI: "Your video stays local while our algorithms transform it into privacy-safe data". Kemtai: "Processing is done on the edge device, and video is not recorded". Spottr's `privacyBlur.ts` pixelates bystanders in the replay: describe exactly that, nothing broader (per project rule).
  - *Needs:* the code must do what the sentence says. Per CLAUDE.md, do not claim more than the code.
- **Pattern B: defaults and opt-in.** Verkada: facial recognition and audio "disabled out of the box"; model training only with "explicit, opt-in consent".
  - *Needs:* a real member opt-in flow and settings. Spottr has member settings, but check whether opt-in is actually enforced for the camera data before claiming it.
- **Avoid.** Certification lists (AiFi: ISO 27001 / GDPR / CCPA; Kemtai: FDA, HIPAA) without audits; they are self-reported and a pre-seed company cannot back them. Also avoid absolute claims such as Flock's 2018 "Your neighborhood 100% owns the data", which later drew scrutiny **[inferred]**.
- Retention: Flock's page names no retention number; Verkada gives admin-set windows. Name Spottr's retention only if code enforces one.

### 3. Two-sided value and who pays
- **Pattern A: operator pays, tied to an operator metric.** EGYM: "higher completion rates, lower drop-outs and stronger membership retention". VPixel: churn flags plus trainer alerts. Glofox and Mindbody: operator subscription with member app as a feature or add-on.
  - *Needs:* a stated retention or engagement hypothesis with at least one operator's own baseline. No camera vendor in this set showed measured retention lift; EGYM and Sency show none that is independently verified.
- **Pattern B: a marketplace that lets the operator pay on results.** ClassPass (incremental fill vs. Groupon's "peaked traffic") and Mindbody's 20% of first purchase capped at $30.
  - *Needs:* real attribution of member acquisition or retention to the app. Spottr likely cannot attribute at pre-seed.
- **Pattern C: members see no change in behavior.** Exersight: members "can train as they do today". Fits Spottr's no-wearable position.
- Flag: Future, Tonal and Hevy are consumer-pays; do not use them as evidence that members will pay Spottr.

### 4. Pricing model
- **Pattern A: per-device subscription, hardware subsidized.** Density ($0 sensor, $45/sensor/month in 2016). Flock ($1,500 per camera per year, 2018; $2,000 in 2019). Verkada (hardware plus a required license).
  - *Needs:* a stated unit cost to serve per camera. For Spottr the unit might be per camera or per gym; no source here shows a per-camera price for existing-camera retrofit.
- **Pattern B: per-location SaaS anchored to gym-software prices.** Mindbody Starter from $79/location/month; Glofox's ABC Insights add-on $79/location/month; Glofox seed-era EUR 80-150 retainer. These are what a gym operator already pays for software.
  - *Needs:* at least one paid or quoted price from a gym. A pre-seed price shown as a plan is fine if labeled as a hypothesis. **[inferred]** the $79/location band suggests the budget ceiling for an add-on is lower than camera-vendor pricing.
- **Pricing evidence is missing for the closest comparables.** VPixel, Exersight, Kemtai, Standard AI, AiFi and EGYM all gate price behind a demo. A public price would be differentiating but needs real unit economics.

### 5. Pre-revenue traction
- **Pattern A: named, small pilots with a scheduled install.** Density 2015: one cafe, Workfrom, and UC gym sensors scheduled for August, with Fortune noting accuracy "has not yet been vetted". That honesty, with named venues, carried a $200k seed.
  - *Needs:* at least one named, real gym or a written plan with date (an LOI or install date), not a logo list.
- **Pattern B: hands-on prototypes placed in real venues.** Verkada's 100 Raspberry Pi cameras given to friends and businesses; Equinox became an 8+ year relationship. Flock's arrest in 60 days **[snippet]**.
  - *Needs:* data from a real gym floor. Spottr's demo clips are the minimum; a live pilot is the real item.
- **Pattern C: usage/waitlist metrics.** ClassPass (reservations 20k to 100k, waitlist, 65+ studios). Strava ($3.5M after 20,000 users).
  - *Needs:* a real member base. Spottr may not have it; do not substitute demo views for usage.
- **Cautionary.** Grabango had many named pilots and still folded when pilots did not convert to paid. Exersight shows grants, an award and one test site but no customers. Peloton's 2011 deck and Standard's 2018 TechCrunch piece show a company can raise on a prototype / "platform is built", but those were different eras.

### 6. Market sizing for venue-based B2B
- **Pattern: locations × annual price per location, then a small penetration.** Toast memo: about 1M restaurants × about $5,000 ARR = $5-10B; "a little more than 1% market penetration" = a $100M company.
  - *Needs:* a sourced count of target gyms and a defensible price per gym. I did not research the number of gyms or the US/EU gym count; that must come from an industry source (not found here). Do not use Toast's numbers.
- Peloton's 2011 deck used a top-down equipment figure ("more than $10.5 billion by 2015"); weaker than bottom-up.
- No camera-vendor source (Density, Verkada, Flock, Standard) showed market sizing in what I opened.

### 7. One-liner / tagline
Patterns, from the earliest stage found:
- **Mechanism plus category.** "AI-powered checkout for retail" (Standard, YC); "an app store for security cameras" (Angelcam seed, Failory summary, **[snippet]**); Owlr: "a single app to view and manage all your IP cameras" (Failory). They state what it is in one clause.
- **Outcome claim.** "The first public safety operating system that eliminates crime" (Flock, YC page). Bold, and "first" is a risk for Spottr given VPixel.
- **Analogy.** "Imagine if buildings protected the people inside using consumer technology" (Verkada, First Round).
- **User benefit in the user's language.** Density's founder: "real-time, accurate, cheap and anonymous".
- Spottr's "automatic workout tracking from existing gym cameras" matches the mechanism-plus-category pattern. *Needs:* one verified working demo behind it.
